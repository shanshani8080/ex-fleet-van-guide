Buying an Ex-Fleet Van: What to Check First

Ex-fleet and ex-lease vans can be good value. How to read the history, inspect the load area, cab and drivetrain, and spot a van that has had a hard life.

Updated 25 November 2026 · 3 min read

Large companies change their vans on a fixed cycle, which keeps the used market supplied with high mileage vans that often have full dealer histories. They can be good value, but they have usually worked hard. A van mot check on the registration is the first thing to do, because a van's MOT record shows how it has coped year by year and gives you questions to ask.

Reading the history

  • Look for repeated fails on brakes or tyres, which can point to heavy loads.
  • Check that the mileage rises steadily. Fleet vans often cover 25,000 miles or more a year.
  • Note advisories about corrosion underneath or around the rear doors.
  • Ask for the service records, which fleet operators normally keep.

The load area

Fleet vans are often fitted with racking, shelving or tracking, which is removed before sale. Look for holes in the floor and side panels and for rust around old fixing points. Check that the bulkhead is secure and the rear and side doors open and close properly, as damaged hinges and runners are common.

The cab

Heavy wear on the driver's seat bolster, steering wheel and gear knob tells you how much the van has been used, and should fit with the mileage. Check that every warning light on the dash goes out once the engine starts, including AdBlue and particulate filter warnings on modern diesels.

Clutch and gearbox

Vans driven in towns with frequent stops and starts are hard on clutches and dual mass flywheels. On a test drive, listen for rattles at idle, check that the clutch bites at a normal height and that gears select cleanly.

Diesel particulate filters

Vans used for short urban trips may not have run hot enough to clear their particulate filter. Ask whether it has needed a forced regeneration or replacement, and watch for warning lights.

Weight and MOT class

Check the manufacturer's plate for the design gross weight. It decides whether the van takes a class 4 or class 7 MOT, which affects where you can have it tested and the fee.

Buying from auction or a dealer

Many ex-fleet vans pass through auctions, where you may not get a test drive. Buying from a dealer usually costs more but gives some protection if a major fault appears soon after purchase.

Paperwork from fleet operators

Fleet vans usually come with good records, because companies have to keep them. Ask for the service history and, where possible, the fleet's defect reports. Large operators often log every repair, which gives you a much clearer picture than a stamped book. A van with no paperwork at all from a fleet source is unusual and worth questioning.

Tyres and brakes

Look at the tyres closely. Uneven wear across the tread can point to worn suspension or tracking problems, while heavy wear on the rear tyres suggests the van has regularly carried heavy loads.

Before you drive it away, make sure you understand the rules on driving with an expired MOT.
